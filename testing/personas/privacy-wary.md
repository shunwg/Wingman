# Persona: Privacy-wary — Mette, 34

## Snapshot
- Age: 34
- Tech savviness (1–5): 5. Backend engineer at a fintech. Reads privacy policies for sport. Runs a DNS blocker.
- Flying habits: Monthly for work, Oslo–London or Oslo–Amsterdam, alone. Long layovers are normal.
- Device: Pixel, GrapheneOS, nothing granted by default.

## Goals
She would genuinely like to meet the right person on a layover — someone in payments or infra. But she will only use a product where she can see exactly what is exposed, to whom, and when, and can prove that the control does what it says.

## Fears and friction
- Asymmetry: any setting where "who can see me" and "who I see" could be different, silently.
- Location. A terminal is a location. She wants to know precisely when it is shown and who it is shown to.
- Anything that reads like a dating app: gender-first questions, photo-first cards, "matches".
- Copy that reassures without a mechanism. "We take your privacy seriously" is a red flag; "only women can see you, and you will only see women" is a mechanism.
- Counts that are too precise. "2 people can see you" on a small flight is a roster.

## Privacy stance (1–5)
5. Everything is a trap until the screen proves otherwise. She will try the most restrictive setting first and see what breaks.

## Voice when annoyed
"Both directions? Prove it." · "That's a location." · "So what does *this* setting actually do, mechanically?" · "That number is too exact." · "Nope." (uninstalls)

## Success criteria
- Every visibility control states the mechanism in words she could verify, including that it works in both directions.
- The gender question says what it is used for, that it is never shown, and that it is never a ranking signal — before she answers it.
- When she narrows who she wants to meet, the screen tells her the consequence for her own visibility, and warns her about the people who have not disclosed.
- She can preview herself as a stranger and see the *actual* redaction, not a description of it.
- Location appears only after she chooses to post an update, and the screen says so before she accepts.
- Every count she sees is a bucket, not a number.
- Deleting the account leaves nothing, and a reload proves it.
